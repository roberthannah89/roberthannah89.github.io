Reading alpine weather

Mountain weather is nothing like valley weather. A perfect day at 400 m can mean 0°C and high winds at 2,500 m. Here's how to assess conditions before committing to a hike.

MeteoSwiss — the go-to forecast

MeteoSwiss is the Swiss national weather service and the most accurate forecast available for Switzerland. It uses a high-resolution numerical model tuned for complex alpine terrain.

How to use it

  • Search for the nearest town or village to your trailhead — not the summit itself, which usually has no forecast point.
  • Look at the 8-day forecast for daily max/min temperatures, precipitation probability, and thunderstorm risk.
  • Check the regional forecast maps for convective activity — afternoon thunderstorms are common in summer and can develop quickly.
  • The radar and satellite views are useful day-of for real-time conditions.

Go / no-go criteria

  • No-go: Thunderstorms forecast for any part of the day, especially afternoon. Lightning above treeline is extremely dangerous.
  • Caution: Precipitation above >40% — wet rock on T3+ routes becomes significantly more dangerous.
  • Good: <20% precipitation, no convective symbols, light winds. Check the summit wind separately (see below).

Summer thunderstorm pattern

In the Alps, a classic summer weather pattern is clear morning skies followed by convective build-up from late morning onward. That means a day can look perfect at breakfast and become unsafe on a ridge by early afternoon.

  • For exposed routes, start early and aim to be off the summit or ridgeline by around noon to 1 pm.
  • If cumulus clouds begin building vertically, or you hear distant thunder, the decision is already made: descend.
  • Do not linger on summits, ridges, via ferrata sections, or isolated high ground when convective activity is possible.

Temperature at altitude

Temperature drops with altitude at roughly 6.5°C per 1,000 m (the environmental lapse rate). On clear, sunny days, the actual surface temperature can be close to the adiabatic lapse rate (~10°C/1,000 m) in exposed terrain.

A practical formula:

Summit temp ≈ valley forecast − (elevation gain in metres / 1,000) × 6.5°C

Example: Valley forecast 15°C at 400 m, summit at 2,100 m:

15 − (1,700 / 1,000) × 6.5 = 15 − 11.1 = ~4°C at the summit

Wind chill makes this feel significantly colder. At 4°C with a 40 km/h wind, the feels-like temperature is around −3°C. Always carry an insulating layer above ~1,800 m even in summer.

Freeze level and lingering snow

The forecast freeze level matters more than many hikers realise. If the overnight freeze level drops below your route height, north-facing gullies and shaded traverses can stay icy well into the morning. Even in June or early July, routes above about 2,200–2,500 m can still hold steep old snow.

  • If your route crosses lingering snow and you do not have the tools or skills for it, choose a lower objective.
  • North-facing terrain keeps snow much longer than sunny south-facing slopes.
  • A webcam check the evening before and again the morning of the hike is often more useful than a generic regional forecast.

Meteoblue — localized detail

Meteoblue provides forecasts at specific coordinates, so you can query the summit itself rather than the nearest town. It's generally considered slightly less accurate than MeteoSwiss but more granular.

  • Use Meteoblue to cross-check MeteoSwiss, especially for summit-level wind and cloud predictions.
  • The multimodel view is useful — it shows multiple model runs and gives a sense of forecast uncertainty.
  • The snowpack / precipitation type tabs help when there's snow risk at altitude (relevant even in June on north-facing slopes above 2,000 m).
  • If the models disagree substantially on storm timing or cloud base, treat that as a warning rather than an invitation to gamble.

Webcams — checking conditions visually

Before any hike above ~1,800 m I check webcams to see actual conditions, not just forecast numbers. Webcams tell you things the forecast can't: is there still snow on the approach? Is there cloud on the ridge? Is visibility good?

  • foto-webcam.eu — high-quality archived webcam images from many Alpine summits and huts. Check the hour-by-hour archive to see how conditions evolved through the day.
  • Meteoblue webcams — searchable by region, shows nearest cams to any location.
  • Many Swiss valley tourist offices maintain live webcams linked from their websites.
  • All of my individual hike pages have direct webcam links for that route's summit area.

What to look for

  • Cloud and visibility: Can you see the ridgeline? Is cloud building from the south or west (storm signal in summer)?
  • Snow on approach: North-facing couloirs and shaded valleys can hold snow well into July. Snow on a T3–T4 route without crampons and an ice axe is a serious hazard.
  • Trail condition: After rain, check if there's visible standing water or mud. Some limestone trails are extremely slippery when wet.
