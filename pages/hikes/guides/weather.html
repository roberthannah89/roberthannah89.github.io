Reading alpine weather

Swiss Alpine weather is violent, fast-moving, and altitude-dependent. A 25°C valley day can mean 0°C and 80 km/h winds at 2,500 m. This page covers what you actually need to know to make go/no-go decisions — based on MeteoSwiss data, not intuition.

Lightning is the second deadliest natural hazard in Switzerland after avalanches — ~2.3 fatalities/year (1946–2015). The Alps receive 3+ strikes per km² per year. Peak risk: June–August, 14:00–18:00.

Temperature at altitude

Temperature drops at roughly 6.5°C per 1,000 m of elevation gain. The 0°C isotherm varies by season:

MonthTypical 0°C altitudeImplication
May~2,500 mSnow on north-facing passes. Many trails still closed.
June~3,000 mSnow patches persist above 2,500 m. Microspikes may be needed.
July–Aug~3,800 mHighest of year. Snow only on glaciers and after cold snaps.
September~3,200 mFirst snowfalls possible above 3,000 m late month.
October~2,500 mSnow reaches 2,000 m from mid-month.

Wind chill

At 0°C with a 50 km/h wind, effective temperature is approximately −8°C. Alpine ridges regularly exceed 75 km/h; Säntis (2,502 m) averages 22 km/h year-round. Always carry insulation above ~1,800 m even in summer.

Thunderstorms

Switzerland receives 60,000–80,000 lightning strikes per year. In the Alps, thunderstorms follow a predictable daily pattern in summer — but they can develop faster than any forecast can warn you.

MeteoSwiss: "Thunderstorms can develop very quickly and it is impossible to accurately predict their strength, timing or location several hours in advance." Severe warnings give only 30 minutes to 2 hours notice.

The summer pattern

  • Clear morning → cumulus build-up from late morning → convective cells by 14:00–16:00.
  • Individual cells last 30–60 minutes but can regenerate.
  • Gusts of 90–120 km/h, hail up to 4 cm, and 30–50 mm/h rainfall are Level 3 (significant).
  • For exposed routes: start early, be off ridges by noon–1 pm.

Go / no-go

  • No-go: thunderstorms in the forecast at any confidence. Lightning above treeline is unsurvivable if it hits you.
  • Caution: precipitation >40%. Wet rock on T3+ becomes significantly more dangerous.
  • Good: <20% precipitation, no convective symbols, light winds.

If caught in a storm

  • Immediately descend from summits, ridges, and exposed terrain.
  • Avoid isolated trees, metal structures, and water bodies.
  • If no shelter: crouch low with feet together, away from rock walls and cliff edges. Discard trekking poles and metal-frame packs.
  • In a group: spread out (5+ metres apart) so a single strike doesn't hit everyone.

Hotspot regions

Southern Ticino (20–30 thunderstorm days/year), Napf area, and the northern Alpine foothills. Inner Valais and Engadine are drier (<10 days/year).

Föhn

The Föhn is a warm, dry downslope wind that crosses the Alps. It can raise temperatures by 14–20°C in hours, then collapse suddenly with a temperature drop of 10–20°C as a cold front arrives. Peak activity: April–May and October–November.

Effects on hiking

  • Lee side (where the Föhn descends): extreme gusts (100–150 km/h in valleys), exceptional visibility, very warm and dry air.
  • Windward side: heavy precipitation, poor visibility, snowfall at altitude.
  • Föhn break: when it collapses, expect sudden cooling and often heavy rain/snow. Do not be caught on an exposed ridge.

Fog inversion (Nebelmeer)

From October through February, the Swiss Plateau often fills with cold, grey fog while the mountains above are clear, sunny, and warmer. Hiking above the inversion — even at 1,000–1,200 m — offers some of the finest conditions of the year.

  • Fog top altitude: typically 600–800 m; with Bise (NE wind), above 1,000 m.
  • Duration: can persist for weeks under stable high-pressure.
  • Temperature: often warmer above the fog than below it (inversion reverses the normal lapse rate).
  • Peak season: November–December. Zürich area averages ~38 fog days/year.
  • How to predict: high-pressure system + calm winds + autumn/winter = likely inversion. Check the MeteoSwiss inversion forecast (published November–March).

Seasonal patterns

MonthCharacterKey risks
May Unstable. Peak Föhn. Snow above 2,000 m. Trails closed. Bridges not yet installed. Cold snaps.
June Thunderstorms ramp up. Wettest month on Plateau. North-facing snow patches. Storms develop fast. Long days offset risk.
July Warmest. Peak thunderstorm frequency. Intense afternoon convection. Heat exhaustion at lower altitudes.
August Similar to July, slightly less stormy. Same as July. All trails and huts open.
September Most stable month. Storms drop sharply. Shorter days (sunset ~19:30). First snow end of month above 3,000 m.
October Clear skies, Föhn secondary peak. 22 sunny days avg. Snow above 2,500 m mid-month. Huts closing. 10h daylight by month end.

Best months for Alpine hiking: September (most stable) and early October (driest, clearest visibility — can exceed 200 km on high-pressure days).

Rainfall varies enormously across Switzerland. This matters for route choice, especially on multi-day planning.

Valley winds

On calm, sunny days, predictable valley/mountain wind cycles develop:

  • Daytime: wind blows up-valley and upslope (anabatic). Develops after sunrise, peaks afternoon.
  • Night: wind reverses — downslope and down-valley (katabatic). Weaker than daytime flow.
  • Notable: the Malojawind blows from Bergell over Maloja Pass into Upper Engadine (reverse of normal), gusting 30–50 km/h afternoons.

How to use the radar

  • Green/yellow cells: light–moderate rain. Usually safe to continue.
  • Orange/red cells: heavy rain, likely thunderstorm. Seek shelter immediately.
  • Stationary cells: flooding risk. Fast-moving lines: wind damage risk.
  • Mountain blind spots: radar can miss cells in deep valleys. Storms may be closer than they appear.

Webcams — checking conditions visually

Before any hike above ~1,800 m, check webcams for actual conditions. They tell you things the forecast can't: is there still snow on the approach? Is there cloud on the ridge? Is visibility good?

What to look for

  • Cloud and visibility: can you see the ridgeline? Is cloud building from the south or west (storm signal)?
  • Snow on approach: north-facing couloirs hold snow well into July. Snow on T3–T4 without crampons/ice axe is a serious hazard.
  • Rapidly growing vertical clouds: overshooting tops on satellite imagery = developing thunderstorm.
  • All individual hike pages on this site link directly to relevant webcams.
