Gear & Safety

Swiss-specific gear, hazards, and emergency procedures. Everything here is based on SAC accident data, Rega operations, and the realities of Alpine conditions — not generic hiking advice.

SAC 2025 data: ~4,000 mountain rescues, 98 fatalities. Hiking accounts for 46% of all emergencies. Over 80% of accidents are falls caused by slipping on wet rock, fatigue, or overestimating ability on descent. 38% of rescued persons were uninjured but unable to continue.

Day pack — T2/T3 summer

Non-negotiable

  • Water: 1.5–2 L minimum. Do not rely on trail fountains (see water safety below).
  • Waterproof shell: afternoon rain above 2,000 m is routine, not exceptional. Hardshell with fully taped seams and a hood.
  • Warm layer: fleece or insulated jacket. Valley-to-summit temperature difference is typically 10–15°C; with wind chill, effective temperature can be 20°C colder than the valley.
  • Sun protection: SPF 50+ sunscreen, Category 3–4 sunglasses with side shields, hat or buff. UV increases ~10–12% per 1,000 m; at 3,000 m with snow reflection, exposure can be double sea level.
  • Navigation: swisstopo app with offline maps downloaded. Cell coverage above 1,500 m is unreliable.
  • Phone: charged, protected from moisture. Install the Rega app before leaving.
  • Food: high-calorie (nuts, chocolate, bars) plus lunch. Exhaustion causes 25% of hiking emergencies.

Highly recommended

  • Trekking poles: reduce knee load on steep descents — descents are where most falls happen.
  • First-aid kit: blister plasters, bandage, antiseptic, pain relief.
  • Emergency blanket: foil survival blanket. Essential if injured and waiting for helicopter.
  • Headtorch: with red-light mode. In September, usable daylight ends before 19:00.
  • Whistle: alpine distress signal is 6 blasts per minute, pause 1 minute, repeat.

T4+ and high-altitude additions

  • Helmet: EN 12492 climbing helmet for rockfall zones and technical sections.
  • Microspikes: 300–400g, essential for north-facing passes with residual snow (common through June).
  • Ice axe: for steep snow on T5+. Useless if you haven't practised self-arrest.
  • Via ferrata set: climbing harness + shock-absorbing lanyard (EN 958) + auto-locking carabiners. Children under 40 kg need additional top-rope security — the shock absorber won't activate.

Clothing and layering

Swiss conditions demand constant adjustment. Temperature swings of 15–20°C between valley and pass are standard, and afternoon storms develop rapidly.

LayerWhatWhy
Base Merino wool (150–200g) or merino-synthetic blend Regulates temperature, doesn't stink. Never cotton.
Mid Full-zip fleece (200-weight) Full zip for ventilation on climbs. Goes on/off constantly.
Insulation Down or synthetic puffy For summit stops and emergencies. Stays in the pack most of the day.
Shell Gore-Tex or equivalent hardshell Not optional. Afternoon thunderstorms are the rule, not the exception.

Seasonal adjustments

  • June: carry microspikes for north-facing snow. Longer routes may still have uninstalled stream bridges.
  • July–August: lightest setup, but storm gear still mandatory.
  • September: warmer mid-layer, headtorch essential (sunrise past 07:00). Gaiters useful for wet grass.
  • October: winter-weight insulation. Expect snow above 2,500 m from mid-month.

Water safety

Swiss tap water and public village fountains are safe. Trail fountains and mountain streams are not reliably safe.

Grisons food safety testing: one in three trail fountains sampled was contaminated with E. coli from livestock. Giardia has been detected in 97.5% of Swiss surface water samples tested.

Rules

  • Fountains marked Trinkwasser or with a filled-glass pictogram: safe.
  • Fountains marked Kein Trinkwasser, Brauchwasser, or Viehtränke (livestock trough): not safe.
  • Unmarked trail fountains: assume unsafe, especially below pastures.
  • Mountain streams: visually clear water provides no safety guarantee. Risk increases after rain and below grazing land.
  • Higher altitude, above all livestock, from a piped spring: lowest risk if you must drink untreated.
  • When in doubt, carry enough from the valley or use a filter/UV treatment.

Swiss-specific hazards

Suckler cows (Mutterkühe)

Mother cows with calves are protective and unpredictable. Attacks on hikers happen every summer. See Mutterkuh Schweiz for full guidance.

  • Never walk between a cow and her calf. Never touch calves.
  • Circle herds from the uphill side, giving wide berth.
  • Warning signs: cow stops chewing, stares, lowers head, snorts, stamps forelegs.
  • If charged: retreat calmly. Drop your pack as a distraction if needed. Do not run.
  • Dogs on leash near cows: cows perceive dogs as predators. If a cow attacks your dog, release the leash — the dog is faster than you.

Livestock guardian dogs (Herdenschutzhunde)

~300 active in Switzerland, protecting sheep flocks against wolves. Shown as yellow zones on the SwitzerlandMobility app.

  • Slow down, stay calm, avoid eye contact. Do not pet or feed.
  • Do not walk through a guarded flock — go around, even if it adds time.
  • Do not hike with your own dog in guardian dog areas. Guardian dogs treat companion dogs as wolves.
  • At night these dogs are especially reactive.

Hunting season

  • September: high hunt (Hochwildjagd) — chamois, deer. Lasts ~21 days, varies by canton.
  • October–November: low hunt — small game, ibex (October only).
  • Wear high-visibility clothing. Keep dogs on lead. Dates vary significantly by canton.

Military training areas (Schiessplatz)

  • Active shooting indicated by red/white flags and red/white balloons around the perimeter.
  • At night: three red lamps in a triangle formation.
  • Check schedules: vtg.admin.ch shooting range bulletin.
  • Unexploded ordnance: do not touch. Report via 117 (police).

Lightning

  • Alpine areas receive 3+ strikes per km² per year. Peak risk: June–August afternoons.
  • Plan to be off summits, ridges, and exposed terrain by noon–1 pm in summer.
  • If cumulus clouds build vertically or you hear distant thunder: descend immediately. The decision is already made.
  • Full thunderstorm patterns, go/no-go criteria, and shelter strategy: see the weather guide.

The Rega app

Install the free Rega app before every trip. In an emergency, one swipe transmits your GPS coordinates directly to the Rega Operations Centre — avoiding verbal coordinate errors under stress. Works with reduced signal (SMS fallback).

The app also has an activity monitoring feature: if you fail to acknowledge a periodic check-in, it automatically alerts your emergency contacts.

What happens when you call 1414

  • Rega Operations Centre coordinates 24/7 from 14 helicopter bases across Switzerland.
  • Target response: any location in Switzerland reachable within 15 minutes.
  • Crew: pilot, paramedic (winch/navigation), flight physician.
  • ~20,000 missions per year; 25% are at night.

Valais exception

In Valais, mountain rescue is handled by KWRO using Air Zermatt and Air-Glaciers, not Rega. Call 144 (not 1414) for rescue in Valais. Rega patron cost waivers may not apply to Valais rescues.

Alpine distress signal

If you have no phone signal: 6 signals per minute (whistle, light, or arm wave — one every 10 seconds), pause 1 minute, repeat. The response signal is 3 per minute.

If injured or lost

Stay put unless in immediate danger (rockfall zone, rising water). Rescuers search from your last known position. Use the Rega app or call 1414. Provide: location name, altitude, weather at your position, number of casualties, nature of injuries.

Insurance and rescue costs

Average helicopter rescue: CHF 4,500. Complex search operations can cost tens of thousands. Swiss basic health insurance covers only 50% up to CHF 5,000/year, and only if the situation is life-threatening.

For tourists

  • EHIC (EU/EFTA): covers emergency medical care but not mountain rescue or helicopter transport.
  • You need dedicated travel insurance with explicit search-and-rescue coverage.
  • Rega patronage is available to anyone domiciled in Switzerland. Tourists cannot get repatriation benefits.

SAC hut essentials

If your route includes a hut overnight:

  • Hüttenschlafsack (sleeping bag liner): mandatory. Silk or cotton, ~100–140g. Huts provide blankets and pillows but not washed between guests.
  • Earplugs: dormitory sleeping. Essential.
  • Cash: many huts above 2,000 m are cash-only.
  • Hut shoes: most huts provide Crocs at the entrance. Boots come off immediately on arrival.
  • Booking: reserve ahead for summer weekends via SAC hut pages. Free cancellation until 18:00 two days before. No-show = full charge.
  • Budget: CHF 60–90 half-board (dinner + bed + breakfast) for SAC members; higher for non-members.
  • Quiet hours: noise drops after 22:00. Use red-light headtorch mode in dormitories.

Trail etiquette

  • Right of way: uphill hikers have priority.
  • Stay on trail: alpine plants are extremely slow-growing. Shortcutting switchbacks causes erosion visible for years.
  • Pack out everything: including fruit peel and organic waste. Decomposition is very slow at altitude.
  • Quiet: no speakers. Many people come to the mountains for silence.
