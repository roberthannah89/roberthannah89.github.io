Gear & Safety

What to carry, what to wear, and what to do when things go wrong. Most of this is common sense, but getting it right is the difference between a great day out and a serious situation.

The golden rule: Turn back before conditions deteriorate — not after. Mountain rescue is available but expensive, slow, and potentially dangerous for the rescuers. Good preparation prevents most emergencies.

The PAEE framework

MySwitzerland and the Swiss hiking authorities use a four-step safety framework. It's simple but worth internalising before every outing:

  1. Plan — Research the route and duration, give yourself extra time, and have alternatives in mind. Factor in the weather forecast, current trail conditions, and your group's fitness level. If you're going solo on anything harder than T2, tell someone your route and expected return time.
  2. Assess — Be honest about your abilities. Mountain hiking trails (T3+) are at times steep, narrow, and exposed. Do not attempt routes significantly above your experience level, and do not do difficult trips alone.
  3. Equipment — Mountain trails can be slippery. Carry the right gear (see checklist below). The mountains don't care that you're only "doing a quick walk."
  4. Evaluate — On the mountain, continuously reassess. Drink and eat regularly. Pay attention to the time and weather changes. Do not leave the marked trail. Turn back early if needed.

Day pack checklist

This is what I carry for a day hike at T2–T3 level in summer. Adjust for grade and duration.

Always carry

  • Water: at least 1.5–2 L. Refill from mountain streams only if they're visibly clean and flowing fast. Carry more than you think you need — heat and altitude amplify dehydration.
  • Food: snacks and a packed lunch. Energy levels collapse faster in the mountains. High-calorie, easy-to-eat foods (nuts, chocolate, energy bars) work well on exposed terrain where stopping is inconvenient.
  • Navigation: downloaded offline maps on your phone (SwissTopo app or Komoot), plus a downloaded GPX track if the route is complex. Cell coverage above 1,500 m is patchy.
  • Mobile phone: keep it charged and protect it from moisture.
  • Rain jacket: lightweight and packable. Alpine weather can change from warm and sunny to cold and wet in 30 minutes. Even in summer.
  • Warm layer: a fleece or insulated jacket — the temperature at the summit can be 10–15°C colder than in the valley.
  • Sun protection: sunscreen SPF 50+, sunglasses (UV400 rated), hat. UV intensity increases by roughly 10% per 1,000 m of altitude.
  • Sturdy footwear: see the footwear guide below.

Highly recommended

  • First-aid kit: blister plasters, bandage, antiseptic wipes, and pain relief. Small commercial kits are available at Coop/Migros pharmacies.
  • Emergency blanket: foil survival blanket, weighs almost nothing and can be life-saving if you're injured and waiting for rescue in cold conditions.
  • Trekking poles: especially useful for long descents — they reduce knee load significantly. Collapsible poles pack down small.
  • Headtorch: in case you're still on the mountain after dark. LED models are tiny and light.
  • Whistle: the international mountain distress signal is six blasts, one minute pause, repeat.

For T4+ or high-altitude routes

  • Helmet: for routes with rockfall exposure or technical sections.
  • Microspikes or crampons: for any snow or ice encountered on high routes in early season (May–June).
  • Ice axe: for T5+ and any steep snow. Know how to use it before you carry one.
  • Rope and harness: T5–T6 only, requires alpine climbing experience.
  • Compass and physical map: for routes above the tree line where visibility may drop suddenly in fog.

Footwear guide

GradeFootwearNotes
T1 Trainers / walking shoes Any comfortable, closed-toe shoe works.
T2 Trail runners or hiking shoes Grip matters on wet stone. Ankle support recommended for longer days.
T3 Mountain boots Stiff sole, ankle support. Trail runners feel insecure on wet rock and loose scree.
T4 Stiff mountain boots (required) No flex in the sole. Correct edging on rock and grip on scree is not negotiable here.
T5–T6 Mountaineering boots Compatible with crampons. Seek specialist advice for each specific route.

Emergency

Emergency numbers

  • 1414 — Rega, Swiss Air-Rescue (mountain and helicopter rescue). From abroad: +41 333 333 333.
  • 112 — General emergency services (works internationally, including from foreign SIM cards).
  • 144 — Swiss ambulance.

The Rega app

Download the free Rega app before every mountain trip. In an emergency, it automatically transmits your GPS coordinates to the rescue coordination centre when you make an alarm call. This is critical when you cannot describe your location verbally — which is very common after an injury in remote terrain.

The app works even in areas with poor cell coverage (it uses SMS as a fallback). It also has an SOS function that sends your location without a phone call.

Rega patron membership

Rega is a non-profit rescue organisation funded partly by patron contributions (around CHF 40/year per person or CHF 70 for a family). As a patron, rescue costs are waived — uninsured helicopter rescues can cost several thousand CHF. If you spend significant time in the Swiss mountains, it's worth becoming a patron. Sign up at rega.ch.

Signal for help

If you have no phone signal: the international mountain distress signal is six signals (whistle blasts, light flashes, or arm waves) per minute, followed by a one-minute pause, then repeat. The rescue acknowledgement is three signals per minute.

Stay where you are

If you are injured or lost, stay put unless you are in immediate danger (e.g., rockfall zone, rising water). Rescuers search based on your last known position — moving around makes you harder to find. Use the Rega app or call 1414 and stay calm.

Trail etiquette

  • Right of way: uphill walkers have priority — the person going up is working harder and it's more disruptive for them to stop.
  • Livestock: give cows and farm animals space; make your presence known calmly. Cows with calves in particular can be unpredictable. Do not walk between a dog (guardian of the flock) and its herd.
  • Stay on the trail: alpine plants are extremely slow-growing and easily damaged. Shortcutting switchbacks destroys vegetation and causes erosion.
  • No rubbish: pack out everything you pack in. This includes fruit peel and organic waste — decomposition rates are very slow at altitude.
  • Silence: in Swiss mountain culture, excessive noise (music from speakers, shouting) is generally unwelcome. Many people come to the mountains for quiet.

Useful apps

  • SchweizMobil App — official national hiking trail map with route numbers and public transit links.
  • PeakFinder App — point your phone at any summit and it names every peak in the panorama. Excellent for orientation on ridgelines.
  • Rega App — free, transmits GPS on emergency call. Always have it installed.
  • Uepaa Safety App — peer-to-peer safety network for mountain groups; sends distress alert to nearby users if you're out of cell range.
