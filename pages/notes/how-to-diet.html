How to diet

What I learned losing 27 lb (~12 kg) over four months at the end of grad school. Lifestyle engineering, not willpower.

  • At the end of graduate school, I had put on a significant amount of weight and decided to lose 30 lb (~14 kg).
  • I had been skinny all my life and didn't know anything about dieting. With a lot of research and trial and error, I managed to lose 27 lb in about 4 months. I've kept it off until now at least.
  • I thought I would write down the lessons that I learned so they might be helpful to other people.
  • The actual facts are generally well researched. I do not have time to find citations, but they are things I have heard repeated over and over in the literature. My tips are more subjective and merely things I found useful.
  • I'm going to assume you have a basic knowledge of dieting and biology so I don't have to cover every detail.

Basics

  • Dieting does work: There's a lot of nonsense out there to the effect that "dieting is impossible", "dieting destroys your metabolism", "you have to exert willpower forever". It's just not true. Lots of people manage to diet successfully and keep the weight off. Some diets are much harder to adhere to than others, and dieting can decrease your metabolism in the short term — but it works.
  • Having a healthy weight is sustainable: Humans were a relatively healthy weight until about 50 years ago. It's possible to maintain a healthy weight without exerting a lot of willpower or grinding at the gym. Living in the 21st century certainly makes it harder.
  • Success in dieting is about adherence: Pursue the diet you can actually adhere to. The specific diet is not as important as picking one you can live with.
  • The effect of genetics is overstated: Just because your family is overweight doesn't mean you have to be. Your environment and habits are far more important.
  • You need to be in a calorie deficit: This has to be the foundation of any diet. There's no way around this.
  • "Eat less than you burn" is overly simplistic: Technically true. But it's like saying the secret to running a successful business is making more money than you spend. How do you eat less than you burn? It also ignores metabolic adaptation: in a 500-calorie deficit, your metabolism will eventually slow down so you lose less than 500 calories of bodyfat.
  • Dieting and healthy eating is a life skill: They can be learned. You'll be bad at them when you start — that's OK. It will get easier.
  • Exercise is helpful, but eating right is key: "You can't out-exercise a bad diet". Unless you're an ultra-marathon runner, you cannot exercise enough to eat badly. Exercise lets you eat more and boosts your metabolism.
  • Having a healthy weight will make you feel much better: More energy, higher sex drive, better health, better appearance. I had high blood pressure before dieting, and it returned to normal.
  • Losing weight to within recommended guidelines makes you healthier: Every 20 lb of fat loss reduces systolic blood pressure by 5–20 points. Reduces joint strain, diabetes risk, sleep apnea risk; improves energy, sexual function, and sleep. Not really up for debate among serious people.

Some helpful principles

  1. Minimize your willpower: Exerting great willpower over a long time is mentally exhausting and unsustainable. Structure your life and diet so they don't require it. If you find yourself needing to exert a lot of willpower, you're doing something wrong and need to re-evaluate.
  2. Consistent small effort: When bicycling, you reduce gears going up a hill — staying in the same gear tires you out. Your willpower is your energy: use it at a rate you can handle. It is far easier to maintain a low but consistent effort than to oscillate between pushing hard and slacking off.
  3. Healthy weight is lifestyle engineering, not willpower: If maintaining my weight were just a constant battle of resisting temptation, I would have no chance. The key is to engineer your life so you aren't exposed to temptations in the first place.
  4. The more muscle you have, the more you can eat: 20 lb of muscle increases resting metabolism by ~200–250 cal/day. You can eat way more than before and maintain your weight. It also makes losing weight easier.

General approach

  • Weigh yourself frequently: Daily weight will fluctuate as much as 5 lb due to glycogen and water weight. Ignore individual readings — weigh yourself daily at the same time and average over 7 days. There is no way you gained 1 lb of fat in 1 day unless you entered an eating competition.
  • Track your calories: Not tracking calories is like shopping without looking at prices. You'll be shocked at how many calories are in foods you thought of as healthy. I recommend MyFitnessPal — scan barcodes for nutrition info. Weigh portions; don't estimate. You'll have realizations like "I really don't want to eat this baklava — it's tiny and I'll be hungry. For the same calories I could eat an entire bowl of beans."
  • This isn't forever: You don't have to track forever. After ~4–6 months you'll know what's in everything. I no longer track. Weight maintenance is a skill.
  • Start slow: Eat at maintenance for a couple of weeks first. Then try a 250-cal deficit. Then maybe 500 cal later. If 500 feels impossible, stay at 250. When I started I jumped straight to 500 and freaked out. Ease in.
  • Avoid crashing your metabolism: When you cut calories, your metabolism slows down, partially cancelling the deficit. This is temporary and reverses when you eat at maintenance again. Frustrating, but the metabolic adaptation will also make you less hungry.
  • Don't cut calories too low or too quickly: The faster you increase the deficit, the faster your metabolism adapts. If you cut too quickly your metabolism may slow so much that you can't realistically stay in a deficit. Recovery can take months.
  • Take diet breaks: Every 4 weeks, eat at maintenance for a week. Psychological boost, willpower replenishment, metabolic recovery.
  • High deficits are too hard: 1000-cal deficits are insanely difficult. Stick to a deficit you can handle. I had the most success around 300 cal.
  • Try low-intensity exercise like walking: Walking and jogging burn the same energy per mile. Running 4 miles a day is hard; walking 4 miles is easy. I lost most of that 27 lb just walking. After work I'll call a friend and start walking — by the end of the conversation I've walked 4 miles and burned 400 calories without noticing.
  • Incorporate lifestyle changes one at a time: Don't try to change everything at once. Add one thing, let it become a habit, then add the next. Sometimes you'll have to step back when life gets tough — fine, get back in the saddle later.

Minimizing willpower

So how do you minimize willpower? Some strategies.

Eat low-calorie-density foods

Fullness is more about volume in your stomach than calories. It's super easy to eat 600 calories of peanut butter; I challenge you to eat 600 calories of carrots — that's 3.5 lb. (Don't actually try — way too much fiber.) The point: you can make yourself full if you eat the right foods.

Eating strategies

  • Eat slowly. There's a delay between eating and your brain registering fullness.
  • Eat vegetables / low-density foods first to be full when you reach high-calorie foods.
  • Drink lots of water with meals to physically fill your stomach.

Foods to avoid (high calorie density — won't make you full)

  • Dense or unsatiating carbs: Bread, cereal, pasta, white rice. Too easy to eat a lot of these. Honestly just don't eat bread ever. You can either eat pizza/bread or be thin — not both.
  • High-density fats: Butter, peanut butter, nuts, olive oil. Healthy yes, but they make adherence too difficult.
  • Processed foods: Chips, potatoes. Potatoes are metabolically equivalent to raw sugar — they don't count as a vegetable.
  • Sugary drinks: Soft drinks, sweets, fruit juice. Fruit juice is basically sugar water.
  • Liquid calories: No protein shakes or smoothies. Least satiating, fastest metabolized.
  • Restaurant foods: Impossible to estimate calories accurately unless it's a chain. The chef cares about palatability, not your weight.

Foods to fill your house with

  • Vegetables: Carrots, broccoli, cauliflower. The easier way to lose weight is to eat a TON of vegetables — you literally won't have stomach space for unhealthy food.
  • Greens: Salads, greens, tomatoes. Don't eat cruciferous vegetables or too much fiber before bed or you'll spend all night farting and snacking instead of sleeping.
  • Slow-digesting carbs: Oatmeal, brown rice. You need a little carbs; pick high-fiber ones for satiety.
  • Lean proteins: Lean chicken, fish, lean beef, beans, lentils. Protein is the most filling macronutrient. Chicken breast and white fish are fantastic.
  • Fat-free yogurt — tasty and low calorie.
  • Sugar-free jelly/jello — eat a shitload, practically no calories.
  • Carbonated drinks — tasty and create a sensation of fullness.
  • Stevia / artificial sweeteners — they won't give you cancer. Don't drink 12 diet cokes per day, but short of that you're fine.
  • Caffeine — stimulants suppress appetite.
  • Low-fat popcorn, shirataki noodles.
  • Interesting condiments: sriracha, soy sauce, tabasco.

Don't let yourself get too hungry

Hunger should never go beyond 7/10. Beyond that you'll burn through willpower or lose self-control. If you're always super hungry, something is wrong. Don't shop while hungry.

Don't let yourself get too full

If you feel "stuffed", you've overeaten. Eat just enough to feel full. Consistent effort: easier to be slightly hungry all day than stuffed half the day and starving the rest.

Don't have anything unhealthy in your house

If you buy a packet of chips or candy, there is a 100% chance you will eat it, probably quickly. Far easier to not buy it than to resist eating it.

Other strategies

  • Cook as often as you can: Easier to eat healthy, control portions and ingredients, estimate calorie content.
  • Snack on vegetables: Keep a bowl of carrots and broccoli at your desk. Lasts you between meals.
  • Avoid people with bad habits: If everyone at the table is eating chips, you have no chance. Suggest different activities, eat before events, etc.
  • Don't go to bed stuffed: Sleep is a great opportunity to burn fat without willpower — if you're awake at 4am you'd be tempted to snack, but you're unconscious. Just don't go to bed so full it disturbs your sleep.
  • Find a physical activity you like: I hate jogging, and I think most people do if they're honest. Find something you enjoy — even less intense — and you'll burn more calories long-term because you'll actually do it.
  • Strength train: More muscle raises maintenance calories so you can eat more. Walking is still the #1 weight-loss exercise.
  • Fitness: Running a mile burns the same energy regardless of speed. Fit enough to run 5 miles in 40 minutes? That's 500 extra calories you can eat that day. Fitness isn't necessary — it just makes burning calories more time-efficient.
  • Eat the portions you need, not what you're given: Maybe you're only hungry for 70% of a plate of pasta. Ask the waiter to take it away. With chips, throw the rest away when you're not enjoying them — otherwise you'll eat the whole bag.
  • Find hobbies: A common reason for overeating is boredom. Find another way to entertain yourself.

Water weight

Weight oscillations on the scale are confusing and discouraging. A big part of the confusion is water weight — additional weight that isn't strongly related to bodyfat.

Salt

When you eat salty foods, the salt propagates through your tissue. Your body dilutes the extra salt by making you thirsty. The water sits in your tissue and can make you significantly heavier (up to 4 lb for me) within hours. That's the "bloated" feeling.

If you eat a salty dinner and wake up 3 lb heavier, don't panic — drink water through the day, urinate the salt out, weight returns to normal.

Glycogen

Extra carbs (pasta, alcohol) are stored as glycogen — carbs bound to water. Each gram of carbs binds 4 g of water. Eat 100 g extra carbs and your body stores 400 g of water with the resulting glycogen. This is the primary reason your weight changes day-to-day.

Glycogen stores have a fixed range based on body size. Once full, additional carbs are converted to fat. When depleted, your body starts converting fat to sugar.

Diet implications

  • If you eat a lot of carbs or drink alcohol one day, glycogen rises and you retain water — a few pounds heavier the next day. Deplete glycogen and the water disappears.
  • The first 5 lb of "weight loss" is mostly 1 lb carbs + 4 lb water. That's why it gets harder after the first 5 lb — that's when you start losing actual fat, which is much slower because fat isn't stored with much water.
  • Don't be discouraged when progress stalls. Lose 10 lb, then a night of drinking puts 5 lb back? You haven't lost progress — you replenished glycogen. It will go away in a deficit.
