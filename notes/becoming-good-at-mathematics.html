Becoming good at mathematics

Some thoughts gathered over the years. Mainly geared towards undergraduates and graduates, but a lot of it applies outside mathematics. Originally written ~2015, with content added over time.

1. The natural state of things is struggle

Mathematics is one of the hardest things you can do. While everything is easier when you have a proper foundation, it will never be easy. For me almost every day is a challenge. Learning new things and problem solving is painful, uncomfortable — even though I've been doing math for many years. However I know that it is hard, and so this fact does not dishearten me: the pain that you feel is a reflection of the intrinsic difficulty of the subject, not a reflection of your aptitude.

2. The major determinant of success is hard work and accumulating deep understanding

  • You have all the brainpower you need to succeed: I'm of the controversial position that anyone can become very good at math (a position shared by Terry Tao). However it takes an unreasonable amount of work to achieve mastery. Every successful student works extremely hard, and anyone who tells you they get good grades without studying is a liar.
  • Grinding is necessary but not sufficient: Hard work isn't enough — it's easy to work very hard and achieve little. You need to emphasize rigor, and understand every aspect of what you do. Never be vague, flippant, lazy, hand-waving. Leave no stone unturned. I recommend my undergraduate students study 6+ hours per week per subject, and study over the summer if they're ambitious.
  • Deep understanding: In the short term, it is quicker to have a shallow understanding of what you're doing. It may take up to 5× longer to understand the material properly. You may be able to muddle through homework, but progressing without a rigorous foundation will come back to haunt you and prevent you from progressing further. A steady accumulation of deep understanding saves time in the long run, and is the only viable path to long-term success in mathematics. There are no shortcuts.

3. Don't directly attack the problem

What are you supposed to do then? Over a long time, I have experienced far more success if instead of attacking a problem, I sought to simply understand all of the background theory. I would explore every avenue, and eventually, through doing this, I would incidentally solve the problem with ease. Directly attacking a problem is often fruitless and frustrating in the extreme. When you have sufficient understanding of the background, the problem will become easy and the solution will become obvious to you.

4. Learn, re-learn, reformulate, draw

I will often spend an entire weekend simply trying to understand a single page of a book. Here is a process I go through: I'll read the proof of something. I'll reprove it myself, following the author's proof as I go. I may reprove it in different ways, from different perspectives. I'll draw pictures of the flow of the proof — what leads to what? I'll ask: What is the fundamental idea of the proof? What techniques are used, and how did I miss them? How would I recognize the way forward if I saw something similar in the future?

5. Articulate your thoughts on paper

  • You should be constantly writing: If you aren't writing, you aren't learning. I use a tremendous amount of paper as I try to develop ideas. I am constantly drawing diagrams, arrows, comments, scribbles. Even as an advanced PhD student, I find it far more effective to learn and research on paper. For the last 10 years I've always carried a pad of paper with me wherever I go. I also remember things I write down far better.
  • Paper complements how your brain works: Apart from computers being inherently distracting, any barrier or layer you put between you and the core cognition of what you are doing has a detrimental effect. Paper allows you to flexibly notate your thoughts in a way that more closely follows the structure of how a human brain actually thinks: nonlinearly, in graphs and networks.
  • Paper brings focus: Writing helps focus your thoughts and augments your short-term memory. Being able to see your thoughts on paper means that your short-term memory is free for better use. Thinking without paper is useful for free-form thought. Both methods complement each other and should alternate.

6. Never compare yourself to others

This is an easy trap to fall into. There is an inherent bias in observation: you only observe peoples' successes and never their failures (and people aren't super eager to inform you of failures either). Call it the Facebook effect. When someone intimidates you, remember that the buildup to where they are may have taken decades, and that you will eventually reach their level over time. Topics you have now mastered seemed so difficult when you were introduced to them long ago — but now they are easy. The same will become true of what you are struggling with now, and what you will struggle with next.

7. Don't work too long

  • Work in chunks: You can really only concentrate on something difficult for about 75 minutes. I have asked many people including professors, and universally, people seem to get work done in small chunks of about an hour. It is possible to work much longer, but observe yourself and you'll realize that you are distracted and have ceased to make any real progress. Take a walk, take a nap — it's more useful than continuing.
  • Reset your mind: It's a trope, but it's also true: you're going to think of your best ideas when you're sleeping under a tree, brushing your teeth, walking around the block. Staying religiously glued to your desk, apart from being miserable, will inevitably lead to narrow thinking, ruts, going around in circles, frustration, despair. Free your mind, get some sunlight, and go for a cycle.
  • Your core cognition time: Work that is difficult — learning new things, research, problem solving — I refer to as "core cognition". Over about a decade I have never consistently been able to get more than about 3.5 hours of productive core cognition per day, and many other people have similar experiences. It appears to be a finite resource that needs to be replenished over a long time. It's possible to do simple things like write slides seemingly forever — but this is not the case for core cognition.

8. Get adequate sleep

  • Give your brain what it needs: Your brain is a sensitive instrument — it needs to be well rested and nourished to function properly. It has been said that Americans don't remember what it feels like to be completely alert. People who are sleep deprived ironically rate their concentration and aptitude higher, but perform much worse in tests. When you get adequate sleep, everything just seems easier. Things that seemed daunting are no longer so hard. If in doubt, I always take a nap and work later: I have never regretted it because I've always returned to what I was doing with renewed vigor.
  • Know when to stop: You have to get in the habit of sleeping more — force yourself to stop, have the discipline to say "no more tonight". When you see the positive benefits, it becomes easier.
